Website Migration SEO Checklist: Protecting Search Visibility During Change

A migration can improve design, performance, security, and operations. It can also remove valuable URLs, break internal links, or prevent search engines from accessing the new website. The difference is usually planning, ownership, and verification.

By SK Khorrum · Published and updated September 9, 2026

Direct answer

A website migration SEO checklist should document the current site, identify pages that generate organic value, map every important old URL to its best new destination, test the staging website, and verify redirects, indexation, analytics, internal links, canonicals, and XML sitemaps after launch. Keep the old data and redirect rules available, monitor performance by page rather than only sitewide, and assign responsibility for fixing issues quickly.

Why migrations create search risk

Search engines understand a website through URLs, links, content, status codes, canonical signals, structured data, and historical behaviour. A domain move, CMS replacement, redesign, HTTPS change, or major restructuring can alter several of those signals simultaneously.

For a business serving Bangladesh, the USA, the UK, Europe, or remote global markets, lost visibility may also affect enquiries across different services, locations, languages, and time zones. SEO therefore needs to be part of migration governance, not a final launch-day inspection.

A practical website migration SEO checklist

Use three controlled phases: protect what already works, validate the release, and monitor how users and search engines respond. A preliminary SEO audit can establish the evidence needed for informed decisions.

Phase 1: Inventory and benchmark the current site

  • Crawl the live website and export indexable URLs, titles, headings, canonicals, status codes, directives, structured data, and internal links.
  • Combine crawl data with analytics, search performance, backlink, conversion, product, and lead-quality information.
  • Identify priority pages by qualified traffic, revenue contribution, enquiries, links, rankings, and strategic importance.
  • Record baseline organic sessions, conversions, indexed pages, top queries, page speed, and crawl errors.
  • Save robots.txt files, XML sitemaps, redirect rules, analytics settings, and representative page copies for comparison.

Traffic alone is not enough. A low-traffic service page may produce valuable enquiries, while a popular article may contribute little commercially. The inventory should reflect business value as well as search volume.

Phase 2: Build the URL and content plan

  • Decide which pages will remain, merge, move, improve, or be retired.
  • Create a one-to-one mapping from each valuable old URL to the closest relevant new URL.
  • Use permanent server-side redirects and avoid chains, loops, irrelevant destinations, and mass homepage redirects.
  • Preserve useful content, intent, headings, metadata, media, and internal links unless evidence supports a change.
  • Review navigation depth, breadcrumbs, pagination, filters, language targeting, and faceted URLs before templates are approved.

Do not automatically delete a page because it looks outdated. First check whether it has links, rankings, conversions, or a role in the customer journey. Where several pages overlap, consolidate them into a stronger destination and redirect each retired URL appropriately.

Phase 3: Test staging before launch

  • Keep staging inaccessible to public indexing, but ensure authorised reviewers can crawl it fully.
  • Compare staging URLs with the approved inventory and investigate missing, duplicated, or unexpectedly changed pages.
  • Test mobile layouts, forms, checkout steps, consent tools, analytics events, and conversion confirmations.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does an SEO website migration take?

A small, well-documented site may need several weeks of preparation, testing, and monitoring. Large ecommerce, multilingual, or heavily customised websites can require months. The schedule should reflect URL volume, platform complexity, stakeholder approvals, content work, and developer availability rather than an arbitrary launch date.

Should every old URL redirect to the homepage?

No. Each valuable old URL should redirect to the closest relevant new page. Sending everything to the homepage creates a poor user experience and may be treated as a soft 404. If no meaningful replacement exists, a genuine 404 or 410 response can be more appropriate.

When should SEO be involved in a website redesign?

SEO should be involved during planning, before templates, navigation, content, and URL rules are finalised. Early involvement makes it easier to preserve valuable pages and prevent technical problems. Reviewing only after launch limits the available options and can make corrections slower and more expensive.

Can a website migration happen without any ranking changes?

No one can guarantee completely stable rankings because search engines must crawl and process the changed site. A disciplined migration reduces avoidable risk, but temporary fluctuations can occur. Monitoring indexation, traffic, conversions, redirects, and crawl errors helps the team identify whether changes are normal or require action.
